Product Engineering — Prototyping

Build the right thing before you build the thing

The most expensive mistake in product development is building something that works exactly as specified and completely misses what users actually need. Prototyping is how you find out before writing a single line of production code. We turn ideas into testable, tangible experiences — from concept sketches to fully interactive UI prototypes and technical proof-of-concept builds.

Fidelity Levels

The right prototype for the question you are trying to answer

Not every question needs a polished prototype, and not every prototype needs to be interactive. The fidelity you choose depends on what you are trying to learn and from whom.

Low Fidelity
Concept Prototype
Rough sketches and simple wireframes that map out user flows and information architecture. No colour, no branding, no interaction. The goal is to agree on structure and sequence before spending time on design details.
Screen-by-screen flow diagrams
Hand-annotated wireframes
User journey mapping
Assumptions register
Typical duration: 1 to 2 weeks
Mid Fidelity
Interactive Prototype
Clickable mockups that simulate the product experience. Users can tap through screens, trigger states, and encounter error messages — without any code running behind it. Realistic enough for structured user testing.
Linked screen flows in Figma or similar
Simulated form interactions

High Fidelity
Technical Proof of Concept
Working code that proves a specific technical hypothesis — an API integration, a real-time data feed, a machine learning inference, an unusual interaction. This is a focused build, not a product, designed to answer one precise engineering question.

Performance benchmarks

Architecture decision record

The Purpose of a Prototype

A prototype is not a draft product. It is a question made tangible.

Every prototype engagement begins with a scoping session in which we identify the specific questions the prototype must answer. Without a clear question, a prototype has no way to succeed or fail — and therefore no way to teach you anything. Here are the kinds of questions we regularly build prototypes to answer.

?

Do users understand how to navigate this product?

Put a low-fidelity clickable prototype in front of five users and ask them to complete a task. The answer reveals itself in minutes, not months.

?

Is this technical approach actually feasible at the performance level we need?

A proof-of-concept build tests the specific integration or computation under real-world constraints before the team commits to a full architecture.

?

Will investors or stakeholders understand this product well enough to fund it?

A high-fidelity prototype that looks and feels like a real product communicates a vision far more effectively than a slide deck or a written brief.

?

Between these two design approaches, which one do users actually prefer?

Build both at low fidelity and run a structured comparison session. The answer costs days, not weeks.

What Prototyping Is Not

Four things we hear that slow teams down

These beliefs lead to skipping prototyping entirely, or approaching it in a way that removes its value.

The Belief

"Prototyping delays the real build — we should just get started."

The Reality

A two-week prototype that reveals a navigation problem saves months of rebuilding. The teams that skip prototyping to move faster are the ones iterating the longest. Speed without direction is the actual delay.

The Belief

"A prototype has to look polished before we show it to anyone."

The Reality

Rough prototypes surface more honest feedback. When a prototype looks finished, users hesitate to criticise it. A clearly imperfect sketch tells a user their opinion is valued more than the prototype itself.

The Belief

"We already know what users want — we have been in this market for years."

The Reality

Domain expertise is not the same as watching a real user interact with a specific product for the first time. Experts make assumptions that go untested for years. Prototyping tests those assumptions explicitly.

The Belief

"Prototyping is a design activity. The engineering team doesn't need to be involved."

The Reality

A prototype that ignores engineering constraints produces a beautiful design that cannot be built as designed. Engineers in the room during prototyping catch technical risks before they become redesigns.

Our Process

From the question to the answer in five steps

Every prototyping engagement follows a structured path that keeps the team aligned on what is being learned and why.

Week 1

Define the question

We begin with a focused scoping session — typically two to three hours — in which we map out the product idea, the assumptions behind it, and the specific decisions the prototype must inform. We agree on the fidelity level, the target users, and what a successful prototype looks like. The output is a one-page prototype brief that keeps the whole engagement anchored.

Week 1 to 2

Sketch and structure

We build the concept prototype: user flows, information architecture, and rough wireframes. This is deliberately rough — the goal is to generate options quickly, not to perfect a single approach. We review with the client and select the strongest direction before moving forward.

Week 2 to 4

Build and refine

We build to the agreed fidelity level — an interactive prototype in a design tool, or a targeted technical proof-of-concept. For interactive prototypes, we include all the edge cases, error states, and empty states that a usability test would surface. For technical builds, we build to the specific performance criteria the question demands.

Week 3 to 5

Test with real users

We run structured usability sessions — either facilitated by our team or observed by the client's stakeholders — using the prototype as the stimulus. We capture findings in a structured format: tasks attempted, tasks completed, points of hesitation, and verbatim user comments that reveal what the data cannot.

Final week

Synthesise and hand over

We produce the prototype handoff package — the prototype itself, the testing findings report, the revised user flows incorporating what we learned, and a set of recommendations for the full product build. If the client is continuing with GreaterHeight into production, we carry this context directly into the engineering phase.

Common Questions

Before we start

A prototype answers a question. An MVP is a product you release. A prototype might be a clickable UI mockup built to test whether users understand your navigation — it doesn't work, it only looks like it works, and that is entirely the point. An MVP is a real product with real functionality that real users interact with and that you are prepared to maintain and iterate. The two serve different moments: a prototype comes first, clarifies what you should build, and then informs the scope of the MVP. Many founders skip prototyping and build an MVP that answers no specific question — they learn the wrong lessons and iterate in the wrong direction. Prototyping closes that gap.

Duration depends on scope and fidelity. A focused concept prototype — a small set of screens exploring one user flow — typically takes one to two weeks. A full interactive prototype covering multiple user journeys, covering both user-facing and administrative flows, typically takes three to five weeks. A technical proof-of-concept, which involves working code rather than a UI mockup, can take two to four weeks depending on the technical complexity being de-risked. All engagements begin with a scoping session that defines the specific questions the prototype must answer and the fidelity level needed to answer them.
