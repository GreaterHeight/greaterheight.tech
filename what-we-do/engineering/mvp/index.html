Product Engineering — MVP

Build the right thing
before you build it right

Most product investments fail not because they were built badly, but because they were built before the core assumption was tested. We build MVPs that answer the question your investors, your board, and your users will all eventually ask: does this actually work for the people it was designed for?

Scope Discipline

An MVP is not a small version of everything. It is the full version of one thing.

The most expensive mistake in MVP development is building too much. We define scope upfront with the same rigour applied to the build itself — and what stays out is documented as deliberately as what goes in.

✓

What is in the MVP

✓

The single primary user journey

End-to-end, working, in production. The one flow that tests whether the product hypothesis is true.

✓

Authentication and account management

Sign-up, login, password reset, and the minimum account settings the user journey requires.

✓

Core data model and business logic

The domain model that powers the primary journey — built cleanly enough to build on, not throw away.

✓

Live deployment to a production environment

Hosted on real infrastructure, with a real domain, accessible to real users. Not localhost, not a demo link.

✓

Basic analytics and error monitoring

Enough instrumentation to know whether users are completing the journey and where they are dropping off.

✗

What is explicitly out of scope

✗

Secondary and tertiary user journeys

Features that are genuinely useful but do not test the core hypothesis. These are Phase 2.

✗

Admin dashboards and back-office tools

Until you have users, you do not need a user management panel. Manual processes are acceptable at MVP scale.

✗

Advanced integrations and third-party connectors

Unless the integration is itself what the MVP tests, it waits. Manual handoffs are faster than automating the wrong process.

✗

Notifications, email sequences, and engagement loops

Retention mechanics matter once you have users worth retaining. They are premature before validation.

✗

Performance optimisation for scale

Scaling infrastructure for 100,000 users when you have 20 is a cost with no return. We build for the users you have, with architecture that can scale when you need it.

Principle 01

Define the hypothesis before the features

We do not start building until we have agreed what the MVP is testing. Every feature decision is weighed against whether it helps answer that specific question.

Principle 02

Production-quality where the user experience demands it

An MVP is not rough work. The core journey must be reliable, coherent, and credible enough that a real user will trust it. Shortcuts in that journey are not acceptable.

Principle 03

Success criteria agreed before the first line of code

At the end of the MVP period, what number, behaviour, or outcome will tell us whether the hypothesis passed? If we cannot answer that before we start, the MVP is not ready to build.

Delivery Model

From brief to live product — typically 6 to 14 weeks

The timeline depends on the complexity of the core journey, the number of platform integrations required, and the speed of decision-making on each side.

Week 1 — 2
Discovery and Scoping
We define the hypothesis being tested, identify the primary user journey, agree the feature list in and out of scope, and establish the success criteria. UI wireframes are produced and reviewed. No development begins until scope is agreed and signed off.
Hypothesis statement Agreed feature list Out-of-scope log UI wireframes Success criteria
Week 3 — 4
Design and Architecture
UI design for the agreed screens, with a focus on the primary user journey and onboarding. Technical architecture decisions: framework, data model, hosting, authentication. The engineering environment is set up and the team begins work.
UI designs Architecture decisions Dev environment Data model
Week 5 — 10
Build and Internal Testing
Sprint-based development. We hold a brief fortnightly review with the client so there are no surprises at handover. Internal QA runs in parallel with development from week 7 onward. The build is ready for user testing before the external review.
Sprint reviews (fortnightly) QA testing Bug fixes Staging environment
Week 11 — 12
Launch Preparation and Go-Live
Production deployment, domain and SSL configuration, analytics and error monitoring setup, and the first user invitations. We monitor the launch week closely and are available to fix any issues that surface in production.
Production deployment Analytics setup Error monitoring Launch support
Week 13 — 14
Post-Launch Review
At four weeks post-launch, we review the data against the success criteria agreed at scoping. We present findings on user behaviour, conversion, and retention. This session drives the Phase 2 decision: validated learning becomes the foundation for the next build.
Analytics review Hypothesis verdict Phase 2 recommendations
Who We Build For

MVPs are not just for startups

Validating before investing at scale is a discipline that applies at every stage of product development.

01

Founders and early-stage startups

You have an idea and conviction. What you need is data. We build the product that gets you in front of users quickly enough that the feedback shapes the full product — not confirms what you already assumed.

02

Established companies launching a new product line

A new product inside an established business carries different risk than a startup. The budget exists, but so does the internal pressure to build everything at once. We help you define a testable scope and move faster than your existing delivery culture would allow.

03

Investor-backed companies with a runway constraint

When time to traction matters, everything that is not on the critical path is a liability. We know how to scope an MVP that respects a funding runway and produces results that satisfy the next investment conversation.

04

Teams rebuilding a product that failed to gain traction

Sometimes the problem is not the idea — it is what was built, or in what order. We start with a user research session on what the previous version revealed, before defining a new MVP scope that takes those lessons seriously.

When You Are Ready

Signs you are ready to begin the MVP conversation

Not every brief is ready to become an MVP engagement on the first call. Here is what we typically see in the briefs that move forward quickly.

You can describe the problem your product solves in one sentence, and name the specific user who has that problem.
You have spoken to at least five people who match your target user profile and can describe what they told you.
You have a list of features you want to build, and you are willing to have a conversation about which one is actually the MVP.
You have a budget and a timeline in mind, and you understand that an MVP is not a discounted version of a full product — it is a differently-scoped one.
You have thought about what success looks like in numbers — not "users love it," but how many users, doing what, in what time frame.
Common Questions

What to know before the first conversation

A prototype is a demonstration — it shows what a product could look like but is not built to run in production. A proof of concept tests whether something is technically possible. An MVP is different: it is a working product, with real users, that tests a specific hypothesis about whether the product solves a real problem people will pay for or return to. It is production-quality where the user experience requires it, and deliberately limited in scope everywhere else.

For most products, an MVP takes between 6 and 14 weeks from a clear brief to launch. The range depends on the complexity of the core feature set, the platforms involved (web only, or native mobile as well), whether integrations with third-party systems are required, and how quickly decisions can be made on each side. Projects with a very clear scope and fast decision cycles are at the lower end. Projects that discover mid-build that the scope was larger than assumed are at the higher end.

Every MVP engagement includes a scoping session to agree what the MVP is testing, a defined feature list (and an explicit list of what is out of scope), UI design for the core flows, development of the agreed features, deployment to a live environment, and a post-launch review session at four weeks. We do not scope an MVP without first agreeing the success criteria — the specific user behaviour or metric that will tell you whether the hypothesis passed or failed.

The application architecture itself is built to last.

If you have a partial codebase, we audit it before continuing — to understand the architecture, any technical debt, and what needs to change. In both cases, we do not bill for time spent correcting what was there before we started. We agree scope and cost on the basis of what we find.
