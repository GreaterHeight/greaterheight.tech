Implementation Approach

Three architectural patterns. We choose the right one for your use case.

Generative AI solutions are not all built the same way. The architecture that is right for your project depends on your data, your users, your latency requirements, and your risk tolerance.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation
RAG: when knowledge matters

The model retrieves relevant documents from your knowledge base at query time and uses them to ground its response. Output is accurate, cited, and traceable to source documents. Well-suited for large, frequently updated document collections where the model needs to stay current without retraining.

Best when
Your source knowledge is large, proprietary, or frequently updated. Answers must be traceable to specific documents. Hallucination risk must be minimised.
Fine-Tuning
Fine-tuning: when style and domain depth matter

The model is trained further on your specific data to encode domain-specific knowledge, terminology, or voice into its weights. Reduces inference cost and improves consistency for narrow, well-defined tasks where you have substantial labelled examples and stable knowledge.

Best when
You need consistent tone or terminology that prompting alone cannot reliably produce. Narrow task with plentiful training examples. Latency is critical and runtime retrieval is too slow.
Function Calling & Agents
Agents: when multi-step reasoning matters

The model is equipped with tools — database queries, API calls, calculation engines — that it can invoke as needed to complete a task. Enables complex, multi-step tasks where the model reasons over intermediate results rather than generating a single response from context alone.

Best when
The task requires calling live systems, taking actions, or reasoning over structured data. The answer depends on information that changes at runtime.
Responsible AI

Generative AI deployed with guardrails your organisation can stand behind

Every generative AI system we build includes a layer of governance that sits above the model. We do not treat safety, explainability, and bias management as optional extras to be addressed if regulators come calling. They are built into the architecture from the start.

This matters especially for client-facing applications, decisions that affect people's access to services or employment, and any context where incorrect output has significant downstream consequences.

Output Guardrails
Validation layers that filter, structure, and verify responses before they reach users
Citation & Traceability
Every claim linked to a source document the user can inspect and verify
Evaluation Pipelines
Automated and human evaluation that tracks accuracy over time and flags regression
Access Control
Role-scoped knowledge retrieval so users only receive answers from documents they are authorised to see
Common Questions

What leadership teams ask before committing to a generative AI build

Model selection depends on your specific use case, latency requirements, cost constraints, data privacy posture, and the need for fine-tuning. There is no universally correct answer. GPT-4 and Claude are strong general-purpose choices for reasoning-heavy tasks. Llama and Mistral variants are suitable when you need to self-host to satisfy data residency requirements. Smaller specialised models often outperform larger general-purpose ones on narrow, well-defined tasks. We evaluate model fit as part of the solution design process and can run comparative evaluations across candidate models using your actual data and task definitions before you commit to an architecture.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation is an architecture pattern that extends a language model's knowledge by retrieving relevant documents from a knowledge base at query time and including them in the model's context. Use RAG when the information the LLM needs to answer correctly changes frequently, is too large to include in a prompt, is proprietary to your organisation, or needs to be sourced from specific documents with traceable citations. RAG is not appropriate for every generative AI use case — for tasks that rely primarily on reasoning over structured data, a function-calling architecture may be more reliable. We assess fit before we build.

Fine-tuning is worth considering when you need a model to adopt a consistent voice or terminology that prompting alone cannot reliably produce, when you need to improve performance on a narrow task where you have substantial labelled examples, or when you need to reduce latency by encoding knowledge into model weights rather than retrieving it at runtime. Fine-tuning is not always necessary — many use cases are better served by prompt engineering, RAG, or function calling. We evaluate whether fine-tuning is justified before recommending it.

No LLM is completely free of hallucination, so the right architecture includes guardrails rather than relying on the model alone. We build systems that constrain the model's output domain — using retrieval to ground answers in source documents, applying output parsers that validate responses against expected schemas, implementing evaluation loops that assess response quality, and providing citation mechanisms that link claims to sources users can verify. For high-stakes outputs, we design human review steps into the workflow rather than presenting LLM outputs directly to end users without review.

A proof of concept demonstrates that an LLM can perform a task. A production system performs that task reliably, at scale, with acceptable latency and cost, subject to access control, monitored for drift and degradation, and recoverable when it fails. The gap between the two is where most LLM projects get stuck. Production LLM systems require robust evaluation frameworks, rate limit management, fallback strategies, prompt versioning and testing pipelines, logging and observability, cost tracking, and regular performance reviews. We build to production standards from the start.
